Liftlog / design concept 02 of 03 · metallic

Anodised

Where Plate treats silver as a flat value, this one treats it as a surface that returns light. Satin aluminium, brushed horizontally, every raised face lit from the same point above. Controls look machined because they behave like machined things: an input is a channel cut into the panel, a stepper is knurled, and a record is the moment the metal catches.

Palette

The same neutral family as Plate, pulled a touch cooler and bluer so it reads as aluminium rather than graphite. The accent is not pure white but chrome, #E8ECEF, because a real polished face never quite reaches paper white, and the small gap is what makes the sheen on top of it legible.

Type

Barlow replaces Archivo here. It is a low-contrast grotesque drawn from Californian transit and machinery signage, slightly narrower and with softer terminals, which keeps a heavily textured surface from feeling harsh. IBM Plex Mono holds every editable number.

Screens

Watch the light. Every raised element carries a 1px highlight on its top edge and a shadow on its bottom, every recessed one carries the reverse. That single rule is what makes the inputs read as cut into the panel instead of drawn on it.

The four decisions

The same four questions Plate answers, answered by a surface instead of a value.

1 — What carries the primary action?

A polished chrome key with a lit top edge.

16.37:1 against the base, and the bevel does the rest: it is the only element in view that looks like it stands proud of the panel. Depth is doing the job hue used to do, which is why this concept can afford an accent that is not the brightest possible value.

2 — How does a record feel like a record?

A specular sweep crosses the row.

A hard diagonal highlight passes over the face, the way light crosses polished metal when you turn it in your hand, and the check inverts to a dark recess with a chrome tick. It is the only animated specular in the app, so it cannot be confused with anything else. One pass, 420ms, and it settles into a brighter satin that persists.

3 — What do the charts encode with?

Outline, satin, chrome.

#7A8085 in the band and chrome for the current week, 3.36:1 apart. The under-band step is an outlined bar rather than a dark fill, because a fill dark enough to sit 3:1 below satin lands at 1.31:1 against the panel, which is invisible. Form carries that distinction instead of lightness, and the legend names all three.

4 — Where does danger go?

A red key, built like every other key.

A #CC4133 to #9E1C12 face with a white label. The figure that matters on a graduated fill is the lightest point, not the average: the first draft ran from #FF6256 and put the label at 2.94:1 there, a clean failure hiding behind a comfortable-looking midpoint. Re-stepped, the worst point on the key is 4.80:1. Same top-edge highlight and drop shadow as the chrome one. It keeps the red you asked for this morning, and the shared construction means it reads as the same kind of object rather than a sticker pasted on.

What this costs you

Gradients and inset shadows on every surface, which is more CSS to maintain and the easiest of the three to get subtly wrong: one bevel lit from the wrong side and the whole illusion collapses. It is also the furthest from the flat, matte discipline the rest of the app was built on.

Rejected

Named so they do not creep back in during implementation.
